## 1. The private nondelegation doctrine

### 1.1 Core principle

The nondelegation doctrine holds that Congress may not vest legislative power in private parties. The private nondelegation doctrine extends this principle: the government may not delegate governmental authority to private entities without meaningful public oversight and retained final authority.

### 1.2 Controlling precedents

**Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238 (1936).** The Supreme Court struck down a federal statute that allowed coal producers to set binding industry-wide prices and wages, subject only to presidential approval. The Court held that delegating the power to make rules binding on competitors to private industry was an unconstitutional delegation of legislative authority. Key language: "The power conferred upon the majority is, in effect, the power to regulate the affairs of an unwilling minority."

**Sunshine Anthracite Coal Co. v. Adkins, 310 U.S. 381 (1940).** The Court upheld a modified scheme in which private parties proposed prices but the federal government retained final authority to approve or reject them. Key distinction from *Carter Coal*: the government, not the private party, made the final binding decision.

**FCC v. Consumers' Research, 601 U.S. ___ (2025).** The Supreme Court upheld the FCC's Universal Service Fund contribution scheme against a private nondelegation challenge. The Court found no violation because the FCC retained meaningful oversight and final decision-making power over the private administrator. The majority reaffirmed that the constitutional limit is on private parties making final, binding governmental decisions without meaningful federal review — not on private involvement in governmental processes.

### 1.3 Application to this investigation

The question is whether any contractor in the FAA modernization program, or in other federal systems under investigation, makes final, binding decisions that the government cannot effectively override. The *FCC v. Consumers' Research* standard is demanding: the mere fact that a contractor performs critical functions, or that the government depends heavily on the contractor, does not establish a constitutional violation if the government retains final authority.

The investigation will specifically examine:
- Whether the prime integrator contract reserves final design and acceptance authority to the FAA.
- Whether any contractor employee approves system changes that become binding without FAA sign-off.
- Whether the FAA could realistically exercise its retained authority given the technical complexity and dependency involved.

### 1.4 Current status

**Legally unresolved.** The FAA retains statutory authority over the airspace under 49 U.S.C. § 40103. The question is whether contractual arrangements effectively transfer that authority. No contract has been reviewed.

## 2. The Appointments Clause

### 2.1 Doctrine

Article II, Section 2 of the Constitution requires that "Officers of the United States" — persons exercising significant authority pursuant to federal law — be appointed by the President (with Senate confirmation) or, for inferior officers, by the President alone, courts, or heads of departments as Congress directs. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976); *Lucia v. SEC*, 585 U.S. 237 (2018).

A private contractor employee cannot be an Officer of the United States unless appointed through a constitutionally compliant process. If a private employee exercises "significant authority" pursuant to federal law — that is, makes final, binding governmental decisions — there may be an Appointments Clause problem.

### 2.2 Application

This investigation will examine whether any contractor employee in the FAA modernization program, or other programs under review, exercises final authority over matters that bind the United States or the public.

### 2.3 Current status

**Unverified.** No specific delegation of final authority to a named contractor employee has been identified.

## 3. Inherently governmental functions

### 3.1 Statutory and regulatory framework

**The FAIR Act (31 U.S.C. §§ 501 note).** Requires agencies to inventory commercial activities and limits contracting out of "inherently governmental functions."

**OMB Policy Letter 11-01 (September 12, 2011).** Defines "inherently governmental function" as one "so intimately related to the public interest as to require performance by Federal Government employees." Lists examples including: commanding military or law enforcement forces; conducting foreign relations; determining budget priorities; directing and controlling intelligence; and determining the content of regulations or other actions that significantly affect the rights of private parties.

**FAR Subpart 7.5.** Implements the FAIR Act for most civilian agencies. Prohibits agencies from awarding contracts for the performance of inherently governmental functions.

**FAA acquisition.** The FAA operates under its own acquisition management system (AMS) rather than the FAR. The AMS incorporates similar prohibitions but their precise scope and application require review of the AMS itself.

### 3.2 Relevant functions in the FAA context

The following functions are potentially relevant to the inherently governmental analysis:

| Function | Why potentially relevant |
| --- | --- |
| Issuing air traffic control clearances | Direct exercise of airspace authority over aircraft |
| Approving system design and acceptance | Determines capability of safety-critical infrastructure |
| Certifying controller training and qualification | Public safety certification |
| Managing operational data and surveillance feeds | Foundational to safe operations |
| Decisions about airspace rules and procedures | Regulatory effect |

### 3.3 Current status

**Under investigation.** The contract tower program lawfully uses contractor-employed controllers for some towers (since 1982, Congress-authorized). The question is whether the prime integrator contract or other arrangements cross the line into inherently governmental functions.

## 4. Administrative Procedure Act

### 4.1 Standard of review

Under the APA (5 U.S.C. §§ 701–706), courts may set aside final agency action that is:
- arbitrary, capricious, or an abuse of discretion;
- contrary to constitutional right;
- in excess of statutory authority; or
- without observance of procedure required by law.

### 4.2 Requirements for an APA claim

1. **Final agency action.** The action must mark the consummation of the agency's decision-making and determine rights or obligations or have legal consequences. *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154 (1997). A general contracting trend or policy preference is not a final agency action.
2. **Standing.** The plaintiff must have suffered a concrete, particularized, actual or imminent injury, that is fairly traceable to the challenged action, and redressable by a favorable court decision. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).
3. **Zone of interests.** The plaintiff must arguably be within the zone of interests the statute protects.

### 4.3 Current status

**Not ripe.** No final agency action and no plaintiff with a concrete injury have been identified. FOIA is the most likely near-term APA pathway.

## 5. Freedom of Information Act

### 5.1 Mechanism

FOIA (5 U.S.C. § 552) gives any person the right to request records from federal agencies. Agencies must respond within 20 business days (subject to extensions). Nine exemptions allow agencies to withhold certain categories of records, most relevantly: Exemption 4 (trade secrets and confidential commercial information) and Exemption 5 (deliberative process, attorney-client, and work product privileges).

### 5.2 Application

FOIA requests are the most likely near-term legal pathway for this investigation. Priority requests are described in 08-foia-templates.md and include:
- The prime integrator contract (Peraton, December 2025)
- The FAA Enterprise Network Services contract (AT&T, August 2026)
- The ground stop root-cause analysis
- Any studies or reviews of FAA organizational restructuring

### 5.3 Contractor records

FOIA reaches only federal agency records. Contractor-held records are generally not subject to FOIA unless incorporated into agency records or agency-controlled systems. This is a significant limitation on records access for private infrastructure.

## 6. The Ninth Amendment

Courts have not treated the Ninth Amendment as an independent source of enforceable rights or a cause of action. It does not by itself give anyone standing to sue. This investigation relies on recognized statutory and constitutional claims if the evidence supports any. The Ninth Amendment is noted here only to confirm that it is not the basis for any potential claim in this matter.

## 8. Standing, ripeness, and sovereign immunity

### 8.1 Standing

Standing requirements significantly limit litigation by members of the public over contracting decisions. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* requires a concrete, particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the challenged action. Generalized grievances about government contracting generally do not establish standing.

### 8.2 Ripeness

A claim is not ripe if it rests on future contingencies. As of November 18, 2026, no specific final agency action and no plaintiff with a concrete injury have been identified. Any litigation would not be ripe at this stage.

### 8.3 Sovereign immunity

The federal government cannot be sued without its consent. The APA and FOIA both contain limited waivers of sovereign immunity. Procurement protests are limited to actual or prospective bidders and must be filed within strict deadlines.
