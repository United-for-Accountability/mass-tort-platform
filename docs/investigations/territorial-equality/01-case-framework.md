# Territorial Equality and Equal Protection: Case Framework

**Matter:** Territorial Equality and Equal Protection  
**Status:** Pre-litigation constitutional and statutory investigation. No complaint has been filed. No defendant or named plaintiff has been identified.  
**Prepared:** November 25, 2026

## 1. Case position

The United States cannot claim and exercise sovereign authority over a population while treating territorial status, date of acquisition, or a territory's political label as a permanent reason that the people's fundamental rights count for less.

The Constitution is a safeguard for persons subject to federal authority. It must not be turned into a status-based permission structure for unequal dignity. The territorial power granted to Congress is a power to govern; it is not a constitutional license to create a second-class zone beyond meaningful protections.

The core position of this investigation is:

> **No person should lose a fundamental right, equal legal protection, or otherwise available federal benefit solely because the person resides in a U.S. territory or because of the date or circumstances by which that territory came under U.S. sovereignty.**

This case will test that principle against actual statutes, agency decisions, documented injuries, and controlling precedent. It does not assume that every territorial distinction is unlawful or that the present record establishes a justiciable claim.

## 2. Legal framework

### Constitutional text

- **Fifth Amendment:** Restrains the federal government. The Supreme Court has recognized an equal-protection component in its Due Process Clause.
- **Fourteenth Amendment:** Expressly bars states from denying any person equal protection of the laws. It is not the direct textual vehicle for claims against the federal government.
- **Territorial Clause, Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2:** Gives Congress power to make needful rules and regulations respecting U.S. territories. That power remains subject to applicable constitutional limitations.
- **Ninth Amendment:** Confirms that enumeration of particular rights does not deny or disparage other rights retained by the people. It should be considered with recognized causes of action and concrete facts; it is not generally treated as a stand-alone damages statute.

### Controlling and relevant Supreme Court decisions

1. ***Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244 (1901):** One of the Insular Cases; part of the historical foundation for differentiated constitutional treatment of certain territories.
2. ***Balzac v. Porto Rico*, 258 U.S. 298 (1922):** Applied the territorial framework to Puerto Rico and declined to extend a jury-trial guarantee under the facts and doctrine then before the Court.
3. ***Torres v. Puerto Rico*, 442 U.S. 465 (1979):** Confirmed that constitutional protections, including Fourth Amendment protections, apply in Puerto Rico.
4. ***United States v. Vaello Madero*, 596 U.S. 159 (2022):** Held that the equal-protection component of the Fifth Amendment did not require Congress to extend Supplemental Security Income to Puerto Rico residents on the same terms as residents of the states, considering the tax and program distinctions at issue. The decision recognizes constitutional review but permits Congress substantial latitude for the challenged federal classification.

These decisions do not support saying that territorial residents have no constitutional rights. They do show that courts have permitted some differences in constitutional doctrine and federal-program treatment. Any legal filing must confront those holdings directly.

## 3. Scope of the investigation

The investigation covers federal action affecting residents of Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, American Samoa, and any other territory or possession under U.S. sovereignty.

Puerto Rico became a U.S. territory in 1898. That date is relevant historical context. It is not, by itself, a measure of any person's rights or a sufficient justification for inferior treatment today.

The investigation distinguishes between:

- fundamental rights secured to persons by the Constitution;
- citizenship-dependent rights established by constitutional text or statute;
- federal benefit and service eligibility established by statute;
- local autonomy and territory-specific methods of administering federal programs; and
- political representation and status questions that may require separate legislation or constitutional amendment.

This investigation does not assume all territories share the same citizenship classification, local laws, tax systems, or political preferences. It asks whether any particular distinction is lawful, necessary, and consistent with equal protection.

## 4. Questions under investigation

1. Which federal statutes, regulations, funding formulas, or agency practices provide residents of territories different rights, benefits, services, eligibility rules, or procedural protections?
2. Is the distinction expressly required by Congress, or was it created administratively?
3. What constitutional or statutory authority supports the distinction?
4. Does the government rely on territorial status, acquisition date, local autonomy, taxation, cost, or administrative convenience? What evidence supports that rationale?
5. Are similarly situated residents treated differently, and is there a concrete person who has suffered a specific injury?
6. Does an available, less discriminatory alternative preserve local self-government while providing equal protection and program access?
7. What remedies are available under the governing statute, the Administrative Procedure Act, constitutional doctrine, or a new parity law?

## 5. Legal theories for review

These are investigation subjects, not conclusions that a court would accept:

### A. Equal protection under the Fifth Amendment

A federal classification affecting residents of a territory may be challenged under the equal-protection component of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The applicable standard of review depends on the classification and right at issue. *Vaello Madero* demonstrates that a court may uphold a territorial distinction under deferential review. A claim must identify the specific classification, the injured plaintiff, and why the government's stated justification fails under the governing standard.

### B. Fundamental constitutional rights

Where federal action burdens a fundamental right, the investigation will test whether the right applies to the person and location at issue and whether the government can meet the applicable constitutional standard. Territorial status alone should not be accepted as a categorical suspension of constitutional protections.

### C. Statutory and administrative claims

If a federal agency excludes territorial residents contrary to its governing statute, applies an unlawful rule, or fails to provide reasoned decision-making, an affected person may have a claim under the relevant program law or the Administrative Procedure Act. Standing, final agency action, exhaustion, jurisdiction, and available remedies must be assessed case by case.

### D. Congressional power and statutory parity

Even where existing precedent does not compel parity, Congress has authority to establish equal statutory eligibility and funding by law, subject to constitutional limits. The legal record should distinguish what the Constitution currently requires from what Congress should enact as policy.

### E. Ninth Amendment and retained rights

The Ninth Amendment informs the principle that enumerated rights do not exhaust the rights retained by the people. Any litigation theory relying on it must be paired with a recognized cause of action, jurisdiction, standing, and a concrete injury. The Ninth Amendment alone is not presented here as an established damages cause of action.

## 6. Proposed legislative language

The following is a discussion draft for review by legislative counsel and affected territorial governments. It is not enacted law.

### Territorial Equality and Federal Program Parity Act

**SEC. 1. SHORT TITLE.**  
This Act may be cited as the “Territorial Equality and Federal Program Parity Act.”

**SEC. 2. PURPOSE.**  
The purposes of this Act are to secure a meaningful federal statutory floor for persons residing in every U.S. territory, establish parity as the default for federal programs, require public justification for any departure, preserve local self-government, and provide effective judicial enforcement.

**SEC. 3. EQUAL-PROTECTION BASELINE.**

(a) **Federal duty.—**Each department, agency, officer, employee, and person acting under color of federal law shall respect the constitutional rights of every person within the jurisdiction of the United States, including persons residing in a territory.

(b) **No territorial diminishment.—**No federal actor may deny, abridge, or burden a fundamental right secured by the Constitution or federal law solely because a person resides in a territory or because of the territory's political classification, date of acquisition, or relationship to the United States.

(c) **Independent statutory protection.—**This section provides statutory protections independent of, and in addition to, the constitutional minimum, and shall be construed to provide the greatest protection consistent with the Constitution.

**SEC. 4. FEDERAL PROGRAM PARITY.**

(a) **Default.—**A person otherwise eligible for a federal benefit, service, protection, tax credit, or program may not be excluded, receive a materially lower benefit, face a materially more burdensome eligibility rule, or be subject to a less favorable funding or procedural rule solely because the person resides in a covered territory.

(b) **Comparable treatment.—**Each agency shall administer covered programs so residents of every covered territory receive eligibility standards, benefit levels, procedural protections, and access substantially equivalent to those provided to similarly situated residents of the states, subject only to adjustments necessary to account for a documented difference in need, delivery, or locally chosen policy that does not diminish a guaranteed constitutional right.

(c) **Congressional exception.—**A departure from parity is valid only if a subsequent Act of Congress:
1. identifies the program and the precise territorial distinction;
2. states a legitimate, evidence-supported federal purpose;
3. explains why a less discriminatory alternative would not achieve that purpose; and
4. includes a public impact assessment and a date for review.

Territorial status, historical practice, administrative convenience, or acquisition date, standing alone, is insufficient.

(d) **No administrative carve-out.—**An agency may not create or continue a territorial exclusion through regulation, guidance, funding conditions, or administrative practice unless Congress expressly authorized that exclusion.

(e) **Local self-government.—**Programs may be delivered through locally administered systems or territory-specific methods, provided those methods preserve the rights, protections, and access required by this Act. Local self-government shall not be presumed to constitute consent to fewer federal rights.

**SEC. 5. COVERED JURISDICTIONS AND PERSONS.**

(a) This Act applies to Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, American Samoa, and every other territory or possession under U.S. sovereignty.

(b) Protections stated for a “person” apply without regard to citizenship or nationality classification to the extent the Constitution or applicable federal law protects that person. Eligibility rules expressly tied by law to citizenship or immigration status remain subject to review under this Act.

(c) Nothing in this Act diminishes any right provided by territorial constitution, local law, or other federal law.

**SEC. 6. TRANSPARENCY, REVIEW, AND REMEDY.**

(a) Within 180 days, each agency shall publish an inventory of programs it administers that provide different eligibility rules, benefits, funding, procedures, or services based on territorial residence.

(b) Within one year, each agency shall publish a parity plan with a schedule, responsible officials, fiscal analysis, consultation with affected residents and territorial governments, and any legislation needed to achieve compliance.

(c) A person adversely affected by a violation may bring an action for declaratory and injunctive relief in a federal district court. The United States waives sovereign immunity for prospective relief under this subsection. A court may award reasonable attorney fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff.

(d) In reviewing a distinction under this Act, a court shall not treat territorial status, historical practice, or acquisition date alone as sufficient justification. The federal government bears the burden of establishing compliance with Section 4.

(e) Nothing in this section limits any constitutional claim, remedy, or cause of action otherwise available.

**SEC. 7. SELF-DETERMINATION.**

(a) Nothing in this Act predetermines statehood, independence, free association, or continuation of a territorial relationship. The people of each territory must determine their political future through a fair and informed process, and Congress must honor that choice by lawful means.

(b) Equal protection, fundamental rights, and federal program parity may not be conditioned on the outcome of any status process.

(c) The federal government shall pursue meaningful federal participation and representation for territorial residents. Any change to presidential electors or voting representation in Congress must comply with the Constitution; where amendment is required, the United States should advance that process openly.

**SEC. 8. EXECUTIVE IMPLEMENTATION.**  
The President shall direct the executive branch to identify and remedy unlawful territorial exclusions, defend the constitutional rights of persons residing in every U.S. jurisdiction, recommend legislation needed to achieve parity, and report annually to Congress and the public on measurable progress.

## 7. Evidence required before any filing

A court case requires more than a general objection to unequal policy. Before considering litigation, the record must identify:

- a specific federal program, decision, rule, or practice;
- a named or otherwise identifiable person directly affected;
- the person's actual denial, reduction, burden, or threatened injury;
- the government actor responsible and the legal authority it invoked;
- relevant application records, denial letters, program rules, agency guidance, and communications;
- similarly situated comparison data and evidence about the government's justification;
- available administrative appeals, deadlines, exhaustion rules, and alternative remedies; and
- a remedy a court has jurisdiction to grant that would redress the person's injury.

No declaration should be solicited or represented as proof of a legal claim without clear consent, secure handling, and a defined purpose.

## 8. Requested public commitments

United for Accountability calls on Congress and the executive branch to:

1. publish a complete inventory of federal territorial distinctions in rights, benefits, services, taxes, and funding;
2. identify distinctions created by statute separately from those created by agency policy;
3. provide a public, evidence-based explanation and review date for every distinction;
4. enact federal program parity where Congress has discretion;
5. defend applicable constitutional protections for every person under federal authority;
6. consult territorial residents and governments on implementation and political status; and
7. pursue constitutional amendment if the Supreme Court holds that the Constitution itself permits a fundamental-rights exclusion based solely on territorial status.

## 9. Current status

This is a public-interest pre-litigation investigation and policy case. No complaint has been filed; no court has made findings in this matter; no specific agency action or injured plaintiff has yet been established in this file.
